Stars are enormous astronomical objects made primarily of extremely hot gas or plasma. They produce energy through nuclear fusion occurring in their interiors. Our Sun is the closest star to Earth and provides the light and heat necessary for life on our planet.
Stars begin their lives inside enormous clouds of gas and dust called nebulae. Gravity gradually pulls material together, causing the center of the developing star to become hotter and denser. Eventually, conditions become suitable for nuclear fusion, and a new star is born.
A star's life depends greatly on its mass. Smaller stars can remain stable for extremely long periods, while massive stars use their fuel much faster. Eventually, stars change as their fuel is depleted. Depending on their mass, they may become white dwarfs, neutron stars, or black holes.
The death of massive stars can produce spectacular explosions called supernovae. These explosions distribute heavy elements into space. Many elements found on Earth were created inside stars or during stellar explosions. In this sense, stars are closely connected to the formation of planets and life.
Studying stars helps scientists understand how galaxies evolve and how chemical elements are produced. Their life cycles demonstrate the continuous transformation of matter throughout the universe.
