The Moon is Earth's only natural satellite and the closest major celestial body to our planet. It has influenced human culture, calendars, stories, and scientific discoveries for thousands of years. Its gravitational pull also contributes significantly to the tides in Earth's oceans.
The Moon became a major destination for space exploration during the twentieth century. In 1969, NASA's Apollo 11 mission successfully landed astronauts Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin on the lunar surface. They collected rocks, conducted experiments, and demonstrated that humans could travel to another world and return safely.
Scientists continue to study the Moon because its surface preserves evidence about the early history of the solar system. Unlike Earth, the Moon has very little atmosphere and relatively little geological activity, allowing ancient features to remain preserved for extremely long periods.
Modern lunar missions are investigating areas that were not extensively explored during the Apollo era. Scientists are particularly interested in permanently shadowed regions near the lunar poles, where water ice may exist.
Future exploration could establish longer-term human and robotic operations around the Moon. The knowledge and technology developed through lunar missions may eventually support journeys farther into space, including missions to Mars. The Moon therefore remains an important scientific and technological destination.
